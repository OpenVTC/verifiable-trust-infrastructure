Concept · OpenVTC / Verifiable Trust Infrastructure

Verifiable Data Rooms

A shared space that people read and curate, and their AI agents use as memory — governed entirely by credentials the room itself issues, encryptable so that whoever hosts it cannot read it, and portable across every place it could live.

The idea

One room, wherever it lives

Teams keep shared knowledge in tools that can read everything they hold. AI agents make that worse: an agent's memory is its owner's most candid data, and today there is no good place for a group's agents to share what they learn without handing the transcript to a platform.

A data room is the alternative: a set of records — decisions, notes, intelligence, working memory — held by some host, but governed by nobody except the people in it. The room is an entity in its own right: it has a DID, it issues its own membership and access credentials, and it can be messaged, moved, transferred, and closed. The host — whichever host — stores bytes and keeps them available. That is the whole of its role.

Why it matters

Benefits

  • The host can be blind, and things still work Membership is enforced through credentials the host verifies but cannot forge or enumerate. At the strongest tier the host serves a room it cannot read, for members it cannot identify — and still rejects junk, meters quota, and keeps backups.
  • Humans and agents share one memory Records are human-readable, agent-recallable, and attributed — down to whether a person wrote it or their agent did. No wiki drifting away from the agent store.
  • Rooms are portable A room is addressed by its DID, not a host's row id. Moving host is re-pointing a DID document; not one credential is reissued. Leaving a platform stops being an exit and becomes a setting.
  • Accountability survives privacy Every room has one always-known owner: the accountable party, the credential issuer, the recipient of lifecycle notice. Privacy tiers hide members from hosts — never the room from responsibility.
  • Standards, not inventions Identity and authorization are W3C credentials from the DTG catalog; group keys are MLS (RFC 9420); the room log is a witnessed, self-certifying did:webvh chain. The one new credential this needs is the VAC — argued below.
  • Honest about trust Confidentiality, integrity, and membership need no trust in the host. Availability does — irreducibly — so the design makes host misbehaviour provable and lifecycle rules explicit, rather than promising a zero that doesn't exist.

Design · membership

The room issues its own credentials

Joining a room mirrors joining a community, one level down. The DTG already defines the pieces: a VIC invites, a VMC attests membership, and both are specified generically enough that a room — a DTG node with its own DID — can sit in the issuer's chair. The room adds one credential the catalog doesn't have yet: the VAC, carrying what a member may do.

Design · visibility

Three tiers, chosen per room

Rooms differ in what their host may see, and the ladder is deliberate about the cost of each rung. Communities set which tiers their members may create (rooms.rego); a personal host answers to its owner.

Design · trust

Three layers, three different answers to “who do I trust?”

The new credential

The case for the VAC

The DTG catalog holds six credentials. Two build edges (VMC, VRC), one invites (VIC), three annotate (VPC, VEC, VWC). None of them confers authority. There is no credential whose meaning is “the holder may do X here” — and rooms need exactly that: read, write, curate, admin, as a grant distinct from membership.

The near-miss is instructive. Communities today express role grants as a VEC — endorsement: CommunityRole. But an endorsement is a statement about someone; a role grant confers something on them. Packing authority into the endorsement slot blurs a line the catalog has otherwise kept sharp — the same delegation is not authority line that reserved the VAC's name in the first place. Rooms are the clean place to introduce it properly, and once it exists, community role grants have a correct home to migrate to.

The VAC also completes work already in flight. We have proposed the VDC — Verifiable Delegation Credential — to the DTG credential specification (draft), covering the other half of the pair: acting on someone's behalf. The VDC says “X may act for Y”; the VAC says “X may do this, here.” The word authority was deliberately kept out of the VDC so that the distinction would survive until the VAC existed to carry it. Together they give the catalog a complete, non-overlapping account of conferred power — who may stand in for whom, and who may do what — and an agent typically holds both: a VDC from its human, and an attenuated VAC for each room it works in.

Why authority must be its own credential

  1. Membership and permission change at different speeds. Promoting a member to curator reissues one small VAC and leaves the membership edge untouched.
  2. Privacy needs them separable — and bound. On the private tier a member proves membership and authority in zero knowledge, with a linking proof that both credentials describe the same hidden subject — so colluding members cannot pool one's membership with another's write access.
  3. Agents must run on less than their humans. This is the decisive one, drawn below.

The ask. One new DTG credential type — the Verifiable Authority Credential — attenuable and audience-bound, with rooms as its first concrete instance and community role grants as its second. It is the deliberate companion to the already-proposed VDC: delegation and authority, specified as the two distinct things they are. Whether it takes the reserved VAC name, or rooms need something narrower, is a decision for the credential-spec working group, made deliberately rather than by whatever the first implementation calls itself.

In practice

What people would do with this

T2 · communityopen

Team engineering memory

Decisions, conventions, incident write-ups — searchable by people, recalled by every member's coding agent with authorship attached. The community archive, done once instead of per-tool.

T3 · cross-communityprivate

Cross-organisation threat intelligence

Analysts from several companies share indicators in a room none of their employers' platforms can read — and whose membership none of the hosts can enumerate. A mirror at each org's VTC keeps reads local.

T4 · peersattributed

Deal room

Two counterparties and their advisers, no shared platform, full attribution inside the room. When the deal closes, the owner deactivates the room's DID and every credential it issued stops verifying — clean-room teardown by construction.

T1 · personalattributed

A share on my own infrastructure

My research notes, my reading list, my project context — hosted from my own VTA, shared with the three people (and agents) I choose, on terms no platform sets.

T2 · communityattributed

Incident war room

Spun up in a minute, sealed from the operator, per-member accountable, exported to the archive when it's over. The members-only audit view shows exactly who wrote what, when.

T4 · peersprivate

Care circle

A family coordinating support for someone — medication notes, appointments, what worked — readable to the circle and their assistants, invisible to every platform in between, surviving any one phone.

Design · guarantees worth naming

The honest fine print

  • The owner is always known. Privacy tiers hide members from hosts, never the room from accountability. This one decision is what keeps an otherwise-opaque system operable: someone can transfer it, renew it, answer for it.
  • Rooms are renewed, not reaped. A host can't judge the value of what it can't read, so it never decides a room is over. Rooms lapse to read-only without renewal, members are notified, and only a long, stated, witnessed clock ends in reclamation — with export always available first.
  • Removal is forward-only. A removed member keeps what they could already read — they held it. What they lose, cryptographically, is everything after. The UI says so, because believing otherwise is the dangerous mistake.
  • Shared content is untrusted input — in both directions. For agents, room content is data, never instructions; recall shows who wrote what. For humans, room surfaces render inert. A shared space is also a channel, and the design treats it as one.
  • Total loss is total. If every member loses their keys, the room is ciphertext forever; a quorum of members can re-admit one of their own, but nobody — host included — holds a skeleton key. That is the guarantee, stated as a cost.

Status

Where this stands

Design revision 3, on the design/community-data-rooms branch of verifiable-trust-infrastructure (docs/05-design-notes/data-rooms.md), with two adversarial security reviews — the first reshaped the design; the second examined what emerged. Nothing is implemented. The path runs: one question to the DTG working group (do VMC/VIC bind to node kinds or to deployed services?), the VAC specification, a host-neutral rooms/* task family, then the tiers in order — open first, cryptography on top of a settled model.

Feedback most wanted on: the VAC (§ above), the witness-selection policy for room logs, and whether the four topologies match how your community would actually use this.

Working title — product naming is a separate, later decision. Concept document for discussion; not a specification.
