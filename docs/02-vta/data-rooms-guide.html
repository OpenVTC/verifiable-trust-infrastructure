Data rooms: creating one, and keeping it

A room is a shared space governed entirely by credentials it issues itself — so it can move hosts without reissuing one. This is the working path through that: what you assemble, which of the four topologies you are building, and every verb you use afterwards.

scope operator + integrator serves open · attributed built on DTG credentials · MLS (RFC 9420) · did:webvh full text docs/02-vta/data-rooms.md

Before anything else

The one rule everything follows from

A room operation is authorized by the credential chain the room issued, verified against the room's own DID. Never by the host's ACL, never by a session, never by a roster. Every call carries a presentation instead of a token.

That is why a host can hold a room it has no member list for — and why re-pointing a service endpoint moves the room, with no credential reissued.

Practical consequence: a freshly registered room is one nobody can act in, including you. Registering it with a host and issuing yourself a membership and an authority credential are two separate acts, and the second is the one people forget.

Ground truth

What works today

Rooms are implemented across vti-rooms, vti-rooms-dtg, room-host, vtc-service and vta-service. Not everything in the design note is built, and knowing which half you are standing on saves an afternoon.

Step 1

Pick a topology

The protocol is identical in all four. What differs is where the room lives and who governs creation — never what a room is. Two of them are nearly free: T3 is T2 plus nothing, because a membership credential binds a member to the room rather than to a community; T4 is T1 with the peer relationship as the door.

T1personal

Your own room host

Members are anyone you invite; you govern, and that sentence is the whole policy model. Your own shareable space, on infrastructure you control, interoperable with every community you belong to.

deploy: the room-host binary

T2community

Inside a VTC

A community-wide library whose membership happens to be the roster, or a three-person share — one model. Members of the room need not be members of the community.

deploy: nothing — a VTC already serves rooms/*

T3cross-community

One home host, many communities

Members from three communities in one room is the ordinary case, not a bridge. Read mirrors are the optional half, and the half not yet built.

deploy: nothing beyond T2

T4peer

Between peers, no community

A room two connected peers both hold membership in, stood up in one action from the edge. Nobody governs it but the owner.

deploy: the room-host binary

Step 2

Pick a visibility tier

Fixed at creation, for the life of the room. You cannot un-see cleartext, so a downgrade is meaningless and an upgrade would protect only what came after while presenting as though it protected everything.

What the host can doopenattributedprivate
Read bodies and titlesyesnono
See which member is actingyesyesno — unlinkable proof
Search server-sideyesnono
Produce a per-member access logyesyesno
Restore the content from its own backupyesnono
Serve it at all, todayyesyesnot yet

The ladder measures the host, whoever that is. On a room you host yourself, you-as-host see everything and already know the membership — you issued it — so the ladder largely collapses there. Owner-hosting is not automatically the more private choice; a neutral host that cannot read is a real alternative with a non-obvious answer.

On the sealed tiers, record keys must be opaque. A key reading decision/acquire-northwind defeats the encryption sitting beside it — structured naming belongs inside the sealed body.

Step 3

Create the room

  1. Mint the room's identity

    The room's DID is its identifier and its credential issuer, so it exists before anything else does. did:webvh rather than did:peer because a room's controller must be able to change — transferring ownership is a controller change, and did:peer encodes its keys in the identifier. Witness the log for any room whose host you do not fully trust.

    pnm bootstrap provision-request --template room \ --var WEBVH_SERVER=https://dids.example.org \ --var MEDIATOR_DID=did:web:mediator.example.org --out room-request.json

  2. Register it with a host

    One rooms/create/0.1 document, signed as the owner: this is the single verb no chain can authorize — the room has issued nothing yet — so the request's own proof is the check, and a signer who is not the owner they name is refused. You bring the roomId; a room identified by something its host chose could not move. The host sets epoch 1, an expiry one epoch lifetime out, and the retention you asked for.

  3. Issue yourself membership and authority

    A membership credential (you are in this room) and an authority credential (what you may do: read, write, curate, admin), both signed by the room's key. Store them in your VTA's credential vault — the oracle finds them by issuer, so a credential filed anywhere else is one it cannot use.

  4. On a sealed tier, form the MLS group

    The group's epoch plus one is the room's epoch, so a group you have just created already matches the number the host recorded. Read it rather than computing it: an off-by-one here seals records under an epoch the host rejects, and the failure reads as a key problem.

Step 4

Add a member, and equip their agent

Joining is a two-party act. Without an invitation the owner could seal a room key into your VTA and you would simply be in, holding keys to material you never agreed to hold — so the invitation is checked, consumed exactly once, and consumed only after the join succeeds.

TaskAuthorized by
rooms/keys/key-packagean invitation — minting retains a private key against a Welcome that may never come
rooms/keys/welcomethat same invitation, consumed after the join succeeds
rooms/keys/committhe MLS group itself — never an ACL of ours, which has no opinion about who a room's owner is
rooms/keys/openthe roomOpen capability — this one is our own principal's agent asking
rooms/keys/presentthe roomPresent capability, plus access to the context holding the principal's key

Commits are not optional, and their absence is silent. Every membership change produces a commit, and every existing member's VTA must apply it. A member who misses one is stuck at their last epoch and can open nothing sealed after it — the symptom is “this record does not open”, which reads like corruption. The VTA reports which epoch it holds for exactly this reason: that turns it into “a commit has not been delivered”.

The grant is the role, not the entry's capability list. The oracle gates read the caller's role: application — what an agent integration is normally granted — along with initiator and admin carries both room capabilities, and reader and monitor carry neither. The per-entry capabilities field is descriptive today, so narrowing it narrows nothing; scope an agent by the role you give it and the context it may act in.

Step 5

Keep it alive

No host can promise to hold every room forever, and on a sealed room the host is the worst-placed party to judge value: it cannot read the content, and inactivity is not worthlessness. So the host never decides. Every state below is computed from two numbers the room already carries, and every one of them is undone by a single renewal until the last.

Step 6

Hand it on

Transfer is the owner acting while present, gated on admin — the same grant that mints epochs, because handing over the room is the more consequential of the two. The host does not check that the incoming owner is a member: it holds no roster and no group state, and a host that refused what it cannot verify would fail every correct transfer. That obligation is the outgoing owner's, who can see the group.

Claim is what happens when they are not there, and it needs three things at once — a nomination the room issued naming this claimant, the room actually dormant, and the claimant's own membership. Each closes a different route to a takeover: a nomination alone does nothing while the owner renews, dormancy alone does nothing without a nomination, and neither helps someone who could not commit once they had it.

Renewing is the whole defence, and it is the same act as ordinary use. An owner who was merely away defeats every pending claim by minting an epoch — which is what they would have done anyway. Nothing has to be revoked and no dispute has to be raised. Security that requires no vigilance is the only kind that survives contact with people on holiday.

A claim hands over a dormant room and leaves it dormant. The new owner's first act should be the renewal that proves they can perform it; if they cannot, the next nominee can claim in turn — the succession chain working rather than failing.

Running a host

What an operator owes the room

  • Availability, and nothing else. Confidentiality, content integrity and membership integrity need no trust in you. Availability and non-destruction irreducibly do — which is why the lifecycle rules above are the ones a host must not shortcut.
  • Network DID resolution is a decision. A standalone room host resolves did:key only by default; serving did:webvh rooms means turning on resolution, and that lets an unauthenticated request make your host fetch.
  • Put it behind a proxy. The room-host binary carries no TLS and no rate limiter, and while creation now requires the signer to be the owner they name, nothing yet governs which parties may create rooms on your host — so anyone who can reach the endpoint can register one in their own name. Every other verb verifies the document's proof and the authority chain.
  • Never log the actor on a private room. That decision is made once, in the crate both hosts share, precisely because every host's logging wants to write the acting DID — and on a private room that single line hands you the membership the room was built never to disclose, assembled one entry at a time, with nothing breaking.
  • Back up what only you hold. A VTC captures the room keyspaces in its export; a standalone host's data directory is yours to back up. And a member's VTA backup must carry their room groups — restore one without them and every sealed room they belong to is unreadable, with no recovery but a fresh invitation from every owner.
